## How this dataset was built

- **Scope.** 17 taxa (`SPECIES_IDS`), one module per taxon in `src/data/species/`, each with exactly one flagship
  specimen. Every lesion cites at least one source and carries an evidence level (`established`, `probable`,
  `debated`). Every `debated` lesion explains both sides in `debateNote`.
- **No fabrication.** Unknown numbers are `null`, never 0 or a guess. The only zeros in the dataset are the
  `rangeKa.end` of the two living species. Catalogue numbers, dates and authors were included only when the
  curators were confident of them. Anything uncertain was omitted, moved into explanatory text, or downgraded
  (see the verification log).
- **Ranges.** `brainCc`, `statureCm` and `massKg` give published single estimates (`best`) or published ranges
  (`min`/`max`). For several australopiths and early *Homo*, `min`/`max` are the female and male means
  (McHenry 1991, 1992), not population extremes. Each species' `overview.clinician` states what the numbers mean.
  `rangeKa` is first/last appearance in ka. For *Pan troglodytes* it reflects the first fossil appearance of
  the genus (~545 ka), not the 6–8 Ma lineage split.
- **Placement decisions.**
  - The ~430 ka Sima de los Huesos hominins are placed in *H. neanderthalensis*, because their cranial and dental
    features (Arsuaga et al. 2014) and nuclear DNA (Meyer et al. 2016) group them with Neanderthals.
  - The Harbin cranium is placed with the Denisovans, following the 2025 palaeoproteomic and dental-calculus mtDNA
    results; the name *Homo longi* is discussed in the text.
  - Denisova 11 ("Denny") is a specimen in the Denisovan entry and is mentioned only in text elsewhere.
- **Lesion geometry.** Lesions use the shared site conventions (`src/anatomy/sites.ts`): `t`/`azimuthDeg` for
  shafts, named anchors for skull, mandible, pelvis, vertebrae and scapula, and FDI numbers for teeth. Each lesion
  sits on one bone, and multi-bone injuries are split into related lesions. Where a source does not give an exact
  position, the hotspot is placed on the correct bone and the text says the position is approximate. `render`
  values are sculpting hints, not measurements.

### Verification status (important)

During this curation session the curators could not reach the network. `search_web` returned no results, and
`read_url_content` permission prompts to Crossref, doi.org and journal sites timed out. Consequences:

1. **DOIs** are included only where at least one curator recalled them with high confidence. Where curators'
   recollections disagreed, the DOI was removed (e.g., `trinkaus2017`).
2. **Every citation without a DOI** carries a Google Scholar *search* URL for its exact title. This is a search
   link, not a claimed landing page, so it cannot point to the wrong paper.
3. **Facts** were cross-checked between independent curators. Disputed or low-confidence items were dropped,
   moved to text, or downgraded (`probable`/`debated`).
4. The re-verification queue at the end of this file lists what to check first once Crossref is reachable.
5. The `smithsonian-hop` citation (Smithsonian Human Origins species pages) uses the curation year (2026) because
   the pages are undated. Body-size values attributed to it were recalled, not re-read live, and are queued for
   re-checking.

## Claims marked `debated`

- **`al-288-1-right-proximal-humerus`** (A.L. 288-1, *Australopithecus afarensis*): Compressive fracture of the proximal right humerus. Kappelman et al. (2016) argue that the absence of healing and the compressive pattern, repeated in several other bones, indicate injuries from a fall from a tree. Critics, including Lucy’s discoverer Donald Johanson, note that fossil bones often break in similar ways after death, and Charlier et al. (2018) proposed other causes of death. Fracture timing cannot be proven from morphology alone.
- **`al-288-1-right-distal-tibia`** (A.L. 288-1, *Australopithecus afarensis*): Pilon fracture of the distal right tibia. Kappelman et al. (2016) interpret this ankle fracture, together with the shoulder fractures and others, as injuries from a fall out of a tall tree. Critics, including Lucy’s discoverer Donald Johanson, note that fossil bones often break in similar ways after death, and Charlier et al. (2018) proposed a mudslide and/or animal attack instead. Fracture timing cannot be proven from morphology alone.
- **`lb1-pathology-hypotheses`** (LB1, *Homo floresiensis*): Proposed pathological explanations for LB1’s small cranium. Pathology hypotheses were built mainly on LB1 alone: microcephaly (Jacob et al. 2006), Laron syndrome (Hershkovitz et al. 2007), endemic cretinism (Obendorf et al. 2008) and Down syndrome (Henneberg et al. 2014). They are rejected by most researchers because (1) other Liang Bua individuals, such as the LB6 mandible, share the same small size and primitive traits (Morwood et al. 2005); (2) LB1’s wrist, foot and limb proportions are primitive rather than pathological (Tocheri et al. 2007; Jungers et al. 2009); (3) LB1’s endocast shape differs from those of microcephalic humans (Falk et al. 2005, 2007), and targeted re-examinations found no support for Laron syndrome (Falk et al. 2009), cretinism (Brown 2012) or Down syndrome (Baab et al. 2016); and (4) ~700 ka fossils from Mata Menge are similarly small (van den Bergh et al. 2016). Proponents (e.g., Henneberg et al. 2014) argue that LB1’s asymmetry and proportions fit Down syndrome.
- **`cro-magnon-1-frontal-lesion`** (Cro-Magnon 1, *Homo sapiens*): Erosive lesion of the frontal bone (diagnosis debated). A single skeletal lesion cannot establish a genetic syndrome. Charlier et al. (2018) argued for neurofibromatosis type 1, but infection and trauma have also been proposed, and no consensus exists. The Cro-Magnon pathology had been described earlier by Dastugue (1967).
